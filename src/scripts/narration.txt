You spend an hour a day worrying about things that haven't happened yet.
Job loss that hasn't come.
A relationship ending that hasn't ended.
A diagnosis you don't have.
And somewhere around two thousand years ago, a former slave figured out exactly why that hour is wasted, and how to stop.
His name was Epictetus.
He wasn't a philosopher in a toga giving speeches to senators.
He was owned by a man who once broke his leg, just to see if he'd complain.
He didn't.
Not because he didn't feel the pain, but because he'd already figured out the one distinction that separates people who suffer needlessly from people who don't.
Here it is.
Some things are up to you.
Everything else isn't.
That's the whole idea.
It sounds too simple to matter.
It isn't.
Epictetus called it the dichotomy of control.
Not a suggestion.
Not a mindset hack.
A hard line drawn straight through every problem you will ever have.
On one side: your judgments, your choices, your effort, your reactions.
On the other side: literally everything else.
Other people's opinions.
The economy.
Whether it rains tomorrow.
Whether someone loves you back.
Whether the plane is delayed.
Your anxiety doesn't come from the future.
It comes from treating things on the wrong side of that line as if they belonged on your side.
Think about the last time you lost sleep over something.
Chances are, it wasn't really about the event itself.
It was about your imagined control over an outcome that was never yours to control in the first place.
You weren't anxious about the interview.
You were anxious about whether they'd like you, a decision that was never yours to make.
Let's make this concrete.
Say you have a big presentation tomorrow.
Here's what's actually yours: how well you prepare, how clearly you explain your points, whether you show up on time.
Here's what was never yours: whether your boss is in a bad mood that morning, whether a competitor's product just launched and stole the room's attention, whether someone's phone goes off in the middle of your best slide.
Most people spend the whole night before rehearsing the second list.
The Stoics spent their energy exclusively on the first.
Marcus Aurelius, a couple hundred years after Epictetus, was the most powerful man in the world, and he still wrote this exact idea to himself, over and over, in a journal he never meant anyone else to read.
Not as a general life philosophy for the public.
As a private reminder, because even an emperor forgot it constantly.
If the most powerful man on earth needed to remind himself daily, you're allowed to need reminding too.
Here's where most people misunderstand this idea, and where it stops working if you get it wrong.
This isn't about not caring.
It's not about pretending outcomes don't matter.
You're still allowed to want the job.
You're still allowed to want the relationship to work.
The Stoics weren't emotionless robots — that's a myth built by people who never actually read them.
The shift is smaller and more precise than that.
You do the parts that are yours completely.
Full effort, full attention, no half measures.
And then, the second it leaves your hands, you let it go.
Not because you don't care, but because caring about something you can't control was never going to change the outcome.
It just added suffering on top of whatever happens anyway.
There's a specific practice that makes this concrete, and it's the one that actually ended my own anxiety loop, not just the idea in theory.
It's called premeditatio malorum.
Premeditation of evils.
Before something important, you spend a few minutes actually imagining it going wrong.
Not to manifest it.
Not to spiral.
To rehearse it.
You picture the rejection.
You picture the awkward silence.
You picture the worst version of tomorrow, on purpose, calmly, for two minutes.
And here's what happens when you actually do this instead of just avoiding the thought: the fear shrinks.
Not because the outcome got less bad.
Because you already survived it once, in your head, and found out you were still standing.
This is the opposite of what everyone tells you to do.
Everyone says think positive, visualize success.
Stoicism says, go look at the worst case directly, without blinking, and you'll realize it's smaller than the anxiety about it.
Here's the part that would probably surprise Epictetus.
Modern psychology accidentally proved him right, two thousand years later, without meaning to.
Researchers studying anxiety disorders found something almost identical to premeditatio malorum, and gave it a clinical name: decatastrophizing.
Ask someone what's the worst that could actually happen, walk them through it in specific detail instead of vague dread, and their anxiety measurably drops.
Not because the therapist talked them out of caring.
Because vague fear is infinite, and specific fear has an edge to it.
Once you can see the edge, you can survive it.
There's a second technique that pairs with this one, and it's even simpler.
The Stoics called it the view from above.
Whenever a problem feels unbearably large, you zoom out.
Not metaphorically — actually picture it.
You're not just in your office worrying about an email.
You're a person, in a building, in a city, on a planet, spinning through space, and this email will not exist in anyone's memory in five years.
This isn't about making your problems feel small in a dismissive way.
It's about putting them at the right scale, because anxiety survives on artificially inflated stakes.
Combine the two, and you get something practical instead of just comforting.
When something's eating at you: first, shrink it down to size with the view from above.
Then, if it still matters after that — and sometimes it genuinely does — rehearse the worst case directly instead of avoiding it.
Most anxiety can't survive both steps.
It needs vagueness and inflated stakes to stay alive, and you've just removed both.
One more thing worth naming, because people often ask this once they start practicing the dichotomy of control: what about the things that are partly yours and partly not?
A relationship isn't fully in your control, but your half of it is.
A job interview outcome isn't yours, but your preparation is.
The Stoics had an answer for this too — you simply split it.
You give complete effort to your half, and complete release to the other half, in the same breath.
Not sequentially.
Simultaneously.
That's the actual skill being trained here, and it does get easier with repetition, the same way any skill does.
Try this today, with something specific you're actually worried about right now.
Write down the outcome you can control.
Usually it's one sentence: I will prepare well, I will be honest, I will show up.
Then write down everything else about that situation, everything you don't control.
It's usually a much longer list.
That imbalance is the whole problem.
You've been spending all your energy on the long list, and almost none on the short one.
The short list is the only one that was ever yours.
Epictetus ended up founding his own school, a free man, teaching this same idea to people who had never known slavery a day in their life, because it turns out the distinction between what's yours and what isn't has nothing to do with your circumstances.
It has to do with where you keep putting your attention.
You don't need to control the future to stop being afraid of it.
You just need to stop pretending it was ever yours to control in the first place.
